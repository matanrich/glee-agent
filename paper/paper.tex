\begin{abstract}
We describe \textbf{Equilibrist} (GLEE agent \texttt{d29d57fd038c}), a fully
rule-based agent --- no LLM in the decision loop --- that reached the top
quintile of the GLEE Competition leaderboard against a field dominated by
LLM-driven agents. Its central design decision follows from the competition's
scoring rule: each game is scored as the \emph{payoff percentile within its
exact configuration and role}, seeded by the public GLEE research dataset.
This turns the competition from a sequence of economic games into a
distribution-targeting problem. We embed per-configuration payoff quantiles
computed from 77{,}467 published GLEE games directly into the agent and aim
each offer and acceptance decision at an explicit percentile target. We report
three behavioral case studies in which classical equilibrium reasoning was
measurably wrong for this objective --- including a backward-induction parity
artifact that talked an inflation-immune player into round-two lowballs ---
and describe the transcript-level instrumentation that caught each failure
within hours. Our development loop, per-configuration percentile scoring of
the agent's own games against the reference pool, doubles as the behavior
analysis the paper track asks for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

GLEE~\citep{shapira2024glee} evaluates agents in three families of two-player
language-based economic games --- bargaining, negotiation, and persuasion ---
across a grid of 960 configurations (stakes, discount factors, horizons,
information conditions). The 2026 competition matches agents and humans in a
shared pool and ranks each account by a rating derived from per-game scores.

Most competitive entries wrap a large language model around the game state.
We took the opposite bet: a small, fully deterministic rule-based agent whose
every move computes in microseconds. The bet was motivated by three
observations. First, the published GLEE results themselves document systematic
strategic weaknesses of LLM players (fairness anchoring, premature acceptance,
poor discounting arithmetic), so the marginal value of language understanding
is smallest exactly where the payoffs are decided. Second, a rule-based agent
never times out, never emits an unparsable action, and costs nothing to run at
the volume the rating system demands. Third --- the observation this paper is
about --- the scoring rule rewards something subtly different from playing the
games well, and a deterministic agent can optimize for it directly.

\section{The scoring rule is the game}
\label{sec:scoring}

After each game, the platform converts the agent's payoff into a percentile
against every payoff earned \emph{on the same configuration in the same role},
a pool seeded by the GLEE research dataset and joined by competition games as
they are played; an opponent-strength adjustment is applied on top. The
percentile maps linearly onto a game rating, and the account rating is an
exponential moving average of game ratings.

The objective is therefore not ``maximize expected payoff'' and certainly not
``play the subgame-perfect equilibrium''; it is \textbf{land high in an
empirical payoff distribution generated mostly by LLM players}. We
operationalized this directly. From the public dataset
(77{,}467 games, all three families) we computed, for every configuration and
role, the payoff quantiles $q_{10},q_{25},q_{40},q_{50},q_{60},q_{75},q_{90}$,
plus positive-outcome-conditional variants. The agent ships this table
($\sim$650\,KB of JSON) and matches each live game to it on every
configuration field its (information-filtered) view exposes, aggregating over
hidden fields weighted by pool size.

Decisions then reference the targets explicitly. In bargaining, the opening
demand is the nominal share whose payoff equals $q_{90}$, conceding one
quantile per rejection; an incoming offer is accepted when its
\emph{discounted} value clears a declining quantile ladder ($q_{75}$ in rounds
1--2 down to $q_{10}$ by round 14), bounded by a haircut equilibrium
continuation value (Section~\ref{sec:cases}). In negotiation, aspiration
levels use positive-outcome quantiles so configurations where trade is
impossible (and the whole pool scores zero) cannot dilute the target, blended
with a take-it-or-leave-it optimum $(\,\overline{b}+v\,)/2$ when the prompt
states the opponent's valuation range. Persuasion needs no quantile table: the
buyer computes exact posteriors (Section~\ref{sec:cases}), and the seller
plays honest signaling with a defection window sized by what a lie risks,
$\textit{rounds left} \times p < 1.5$ expected remaining high-quality sales.

\section{What the data taught us: three case studies}
\label{sec:cases}

Our development loop was: play a few hundred games, fetch the transcripts,
score every game against the dataset pools, and read the worst configuration
clusters. Each fix below was found this way, within hours of being deployed.

\textbf{(1) Weak-side equilibrium play is a percentile disaster.}
In bargaining configurations where the opponent is inflation-immune
($\delta_{\mathrm{opp}}{=}1$) Rubinstein logic assigns the impatient player a
small share, and our first agent dutifully demanded 35\% and settled low
--- scoring at the 25th percentile. The pool tells a different story: the
median dataset player in those configurations still secures $\approx$47--50\%
of the pie, because LLM opponents accept fair splits regardless of leverage.
Fairness-anchored demands raised the same games to above-median scores.
The theory was not wrong about the equilibrium; it was wrong about the
population.

\textbf{(2) A backward-induction parity artifact.}
Our acceptance rule compared offers to $0.8\times$ the equilibrium
continuation value. With \emph{both} discount factors at 1.0 and a known even
horizon, finite-horizon induction degenerates into an alternating
last-proposer-takes-all chain, valuing the responder's continuation at
$\approx$10\% of the pie --- so the agent, holding the strongest seat in the
game, accepted 40/100 splits in round two (5th percentile;
Figure~\ref{fig:alice}). No opponent in the pool actually plays the parity
chain. The fix floors the acceptance bar at the pool's $q_{25}$ for the first
six rounds and $q_{10}$ through round 14: empirical quantiles bound what
theory may talk the agent into, while late rounds stay unrestricted so
deadlocks still resolve.

\textbf{(3) Known priors must not be updated.}
Our first persuasion buyer treated bad purchases as evidence and abandoned
positive-EV streams after unlucky draws --- in one game leaving an expected
$+6.7$M on the table after a single loss. But product quality is i.i.d.\ with
a \emph{known} prior $p$: when a seller always recommends, the message carries
zero information and the posterior is exactly $p$; outcomes are evidence only
about the seller's \emph{signaling policy}. The corrected buyer updates only
on sellers who discriminate (sometimes say no), estimating
$P(\mathrm{high}\mid \mathrm{yes})$ from purchase-verified outcomes, and
otherwise buys the prior. Sellers are typed symmetrically: a buyer who
purchases after a negative signal is ignoring messages, and honest
discrimination toward them only forfeits sales.

\section{Agent behavior analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\textbf{Does the behavior match the design?} We verify alignment by
construction and by measurement. By construction: the agent is deterministic,
so a 34-case offline suite pins the intended behavior at decision boundaries
(final-round ultimatums, exact-sum splits, loss refusal, lowball floors,
belief non-updating for non-discriminating sellers), and every live decision
point is reproducible from the archived game state. By measurement: the same
percentile pipeline that drives development scores every completed game
against the reference pool, per configuration and role, so ``is it doing what
we designed'' reduces to reading per-cluster percentiles. This
instrumentation is how each regression in Section~\ref{sec:cases} was caught
within hours --- including one (Figure~\ref{fig:alice}) where the aggregate
family rating was still \emph{rising} while player~1's percentile had
collapsed to 0.07, masked by the other role's gains.

\textbf{Safety and liveness.} Moves are filtered against the server's
per-move field schema; splits are exact-sum by construction; a per-decision
retry ladder degrades to a guaranteed-valid move so the five-invalid-move
forfeit is unreachable. Across $>$37{,}000 completed games the agent submitted
\textbf{zero} invalid moves and forfeited no game by timeout. A supervisor
loop restarts the client on transport errors; abandoned-game penalties (scored
at the 5th percentile) were never incurred after deployment.

\textbf{Observed limits of the design.} Two behaviors depart from what we
intended. First, ratings are non-stationary: because competition games join
the reference pool, the same play earns less over time --- our negotiation
rating gave back $\sim$270 points overnight with unchanged code and unchanged
percentile-vs-dataset scores, i.e.\ the field moved, not the agent. Second,
messages are nearly inert: templated offers with verifiable track-record
claims (``of the 7 you bought on my word, 7 were high quality'') did not
measurably move stonewalling persuasion buyers, suggesting the message channel
matters less than the numeric channel against the current field.

\section{Development process, limitations, and reproducibility}

The agent was developed in $\sim$3 days against the live competition: an
equilibrium-flavored rule-based baseline first, then five transcript-driven
strategy revisions, then the quantile-targeting rewrite, deployed continuously
on an always-on worker. Negative results worth recording: LLM-in-the-loop play
was rejected on cost, latency, and the published evidence of LLM strategic
weaknesses; equilibrium continuation values required both an empirical haircut
and empirical floors before they were safe to use; and persuasive message
crafting showed no measurable effect.

Limitations: percentile targets inherit the reference pool's biases and decay
in accuracy as the live pool drifts from the dataset; hidden-parameter
aggregation (weighting by pool size) is a heuristic; and the agent does not
model individual opponents beyond within-game signals. Reproducibility: the
strategy is $\sim$700 lines of dependency-light Python over the public
\texttt{glee-sdk}, the quantile table is derived from the public GLEE dataset
by a $\sim$100-line script, and both the offline test suite and the
percentile-scoring pipeline are deterministic given the archived transcripts.

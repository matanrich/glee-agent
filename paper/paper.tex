\begin{abstract}
We describe \textbf{Equilibrist}, a fully rule-based agent with no LLM in
its decision loop. Entering on day 25 of the
29-day GLEE Competition, the agent finished 17th of 152 ranked agent accounts
(top 11\%) in a field dominated by LLM-driven entries. Its central design decision follows from the competition's
scoring rule, which scores each game as the payoff percentile within its exact
configuration and role, seeded by the public GLEE research dataset. Under this
rule, the competition is better understood as a distribution-targeting problem
than as a sequence of economic games. We compute per-configuration payoff
quantiles from 77{,}467 published GLEE games, embed the resulting table in the
agent, and aim every offer and acceptance decision at an explicit percentile
target. We report three behavioral case studies in which classical equilibrium
reasoning was measurably wrong for this objective, including a
backward-induction parity artifact that led an inflation-immune player to
accept round-two lowball offers, and describe the transcript-level
instrumentation that caught each failure within hours. The same
instrumentation, per-configuration percentile scoring of the agent's own games
against the reference pool, serves as the behavior analysis this paper track
asks for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

GLEE~\citep{shapira2024glee} evaluates agents in three families of two-player
language-based economic games (bargaining, negotiation, and persuasion) across
a grid of 960 configurations covering stakes, discount factors, horizons, and
information conditions. The 2026 competition matches agents and humans in a
shared pool and ranks each account by a rating derived from per-game scores.

Most competitive entries wrap a large language model around the game state. We
took the opposite bet: a small, fully deterministic rule-based agent whose
every move computes in microseconds. Three observations motivated the bet.
First, the published GLEE results document systematic strategic weaknesses of
LLM players, such as fairness anchoring, premature acceptance, and poor
discounting arithmetic, so the marginal value of language understanding is
smallest exactly where the payoffs are decided. Second, a rule-based agent
never times out, never emits an unparsable action, and costs nothing to run at
the volume the rating system demands. Third, and this is the observation the
paper is about, the scoring rule rewards something subtly different from
playing the games well, and a deterministic agent can optimize for it
directly.

\section{The scoring rule is the game}
\label{sec:scoring}

After each game, the platform converts the agent's payoff into a percentile
against every payoff earned on the same configuration in the same role. The
reference pool is seeded by the GLEE research dataset and grows as competition
games are played, and an opponent-strength adjustment is applied on top. The
percentile maps linearly onto a game rating, and the account rating is an
exponential moving average of game ratings.

The objective is therefore neither ``maximize expected payoff'' nor ``play the
subgame-perfect equilibrium.'' It is to land high in an empirical payoff
distribution generated mostly by LLM players. We operationalized this
directly. From the public dataset (77{,}467 games across the three families)
we computed, for every configuration and role, the payoff quantiles
$q_{10},q_{25},q_{40},q_{50},q_{60},q_{75},q_{90}$, along with variants
conditioned on positive outcomes. The agent ships this table (about 650\,KB of
JSON) and matches each live game to it on every configuration field its
information-filtered view exposes, aggregating over hidden fields weighted by
pool size.

Decisions then reference the targets explicitly. In bargaining, the opening
demand is the nominal share whose payoff equals $q_{90}$, and the agent
concedes one quantile per rejection. An incoming offer is accepted when its
discounted value clears a declining quantile ladder, from $q_{75}$ in rounds
1--2 down to $q_{10}$ by round 14, bounded by a haircut equilibrium
continuation value (Section~\ref{sec:cases}). In negotiation, aspiration
levels use the positive-outcome quantiles, so configurations where trade is
impossible and the whole pool scores zero cannot dilute the target; when the
prompt states the opponent's valuation range, the price is blended with the
take-it-or-leave-it optimum $(\,\overline{b}+v\,)/2$. Persuasion needs no
quantile table. The buyer computes exact posteriors
(Section~\ref{sec:cases}), and the seller plays honest signaling with a
defection window sized by what a lie puts at risk: it lies only once
$\textit{rounds left} \times p$ falls below $1.5$ expected remaining
high-quality sales.

\section{What the data taught us: three case studies}
\label{sec:cases}

Our development loop was to play a few hundred games, fetch the transcripts,
score every game against the dataset pools, and read the worst configuration
clusters. Each of the fixes below was found this way, within hours of the
faulty logic being deployed.

\textbf{(1) Weak-side equilibrium play scores poorly.}
In bargaining configurations where the opponent is immune to inflation
($\delta_{\mathrm{opp}}{=}1$), Rubinstein logic assigns the impatient player a
small share. Our first agent dutifully demanded 35\% and settled low, which
placed it at the 25th percentile. The pool tells a different story: the median
dataset player in those configurations still secures roughly 47--50\% of the
pie, because LLM opponents accept fair splits regardless of leverage.
Fairness-anchored demands raised the same games to above-median scores. The
theory was right about the equilibrium and wrong about the population.

\textbf{(2) A backward-induction parity artifact.}
Our acceptance rule compared offers to $0.8\times$ the equilibrium
continuation value. When both discount factors equal 1.0 and the horizon is
known and even, finite-horizon induction degenerates into an alternating
chain in which the last proposer takes everything, valuing the responder's
continuation at roughly 10\% of the pie. As a result the agent, holding the
strongest seat in the game, accepted 40/100 splits in round two and scored at
the 5th percentile (Figure~\ref{fig:alice}). No opponent in the pool actually
plays the parity chain. The fix floors the acceptance bar at the pool's
$q_{25}$ for the first six rounds and at $q_{10}$ through round 14. Empirical
quantiles bound what theory may talk the agent into, while late rounds stay
unrestricted so deadlocks still resolve.

\textbf{(3) Known priors must not be updated.}
Our first persuasion buyer treated bad purchases as evidence and abandoned
positive-EV streams after unlucky draws; in one game it left an expected
$+6.7$M on the table after a single loss. Product quality, however, is
i.i.d.\ with a known prior $p$. When a seller always recommends, the message
carries zero information and the posterior is exactly $p$, and outcomes are
evidence only about the seller's signaling policy. The corrected buyer updates
only on sellers who discriminate (those that sometimes say no), estimating
$P(\mathrm{high}\mid \mathrm{yes})$ from purchase-verified outcomes, and
otherwise buys the prior. Sellers are typed symmetrically: a buyer who
purchases after a negative signal is ignoring messages, and honest
discrimination toward such a buyer only forfeits sales.

\section{Agent behavior analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\textbf{Does the behavior match the design?} We verify alignment by
construction and by measurement. By construction: the agent is deterministic,
a 34-case offline suite pins the intended behavior at decision boundaries
(final-round ultimatums, exact-sum splits, loss refusal, lowball floors,
belief non-updating for non-discriminating sellers), and every live decision
is reproducible from the archived game state. By measurement: the same
percentile pipeline that drives development scores every completed game
against the reference pool, per configuration and role, so the question ``is
it doing what we designed'' reduces to reading per-cluster percentiles. This
instrumentation caught each regression in Section~\ref{sec:cases} within
hours, including one (Figure~\ref{fig:alice}) where the aggregate family
rating was still rising while player~1's percentile had collapsed to 0.07,
masked by the other role's gains.

\textbf{Safety and liveness.} Moves are filtered against the server's
per-move field schema, splits are exact-sum by construction, and a
per-decision retry ladder degrades to a guaranteed-valid move, so the
five-invalid-move forfeit is unreachable. Across all 70{,}278 completed games
the agent submitted zero invalid moves and forfeited no game by timeout.
A supervisor loop restarts the client on transport errors. Abandoned-game
penalties, which the platform scores at the 5th percentile, were never
incurred after deployment.

\textbf{Observed limits of the design.} Two behaviors depart from what we
intended. First, ratings are non-stationary: because competition games join
the reference pool, the same play earns less over time. Our negotiation rating
gave back about 270 points overnight with unchanged code and unchanged
percentile scores against the fixed dataset, which is to say the field moved
rather than the agent. Second, messages are nearly inert. Templated offers
with verifiable track-record claims (``of the 7 you bought on my word, 7 were
high quality'') did not measurably move stonewalling persuasion buyers,
suggesting that the message channel matters less than the numeric channel
against the current field.

\section{Development process, limitations, and reproducibility}

The agent was developed in about three days against the live competition and
then left to play unattended through the close: an
equilibrium-flavored rule-based baseline first, then five transcript-driven
strategy revisions, then the quantile-targeting rewrite, deployed continuously
on an always-on worker. Some negative results are worth recording.
LLM-in-the-loop play was rejected on cost, latency, and the published evidence
of LLM strategic weaknesses. Equilibrium continuation values required both an
empirical haircut and empirical floors before they were safe to use. Crafted
persuasive messages showed no measurable effect.

Limitations: percentile targets inherit the reference pool's biases and lose
accuracy as the live pool drifts from the dataset; hidden-parameter
aggregation (weighting by pool size) is a heuristic; and the agent does not
model individual opponents beyond within-game signals. Reproducibility: the
strategy is about 700 lines of dependency-light Python over the public
\texttt{glee-sdk}, the quantile table is derived from the public GLEE dataset
by a script of about 100 lines, and both the offline test suite and the
percentile-scoring pipeline are deterministic given the archived transcripts.
